The closing lecture doesn't introduce new mechanics — it asks what to do with
all of the previous eleven weeks, each of which introduced another way a
small account can lose capital or be forced to stop trading. It walks
through what a survival rulebook needs to cover (instrument choice,
position sizing, invalidation, leverage limits, and a rule for when to stop
trading altogether) and previews the shape of the Personal Market Rulebook
assessment.

## The question this week answers

Of every way this semester showed a small account losing capital or being
forced out of a position, what would actually survive being turned into a
rule you'd follow with real money on the line?

## By the end of this lecture

- revisit every instrument covered this semester with a stated personal
  stance on each
- write a risk rule specific enough to be checked, not just aspirational
- identify a style or instrument that isn't a good fit for yourself, and say
  why
- structure a personal rulebook, ready to turn into the Personal Market
  Rulebook assessment

## Key ideas

A **rulebook**, in the sense this course uses the word, is not a summary of
what was taught — it's a set of decisions: which instruments from Weeks
1–11 you'd actually use (and which you'd restrict or avoid), how you'd
size a position (Week 5's method still applies unchanged, regardless of
instrument), what maximum leverage you'd allow yourself on the instruments
that carry it (Weeks 8–11), what would invalidate a position, and under
what conditions you'd stop trading altogether, even temporarily. A rule
that can't be checked against a specific situation isn't finished yet.

Rules also need to work **together**, not just individually — a risk budget
usually caps total risk across every open position at once, not just risk
per trade, because several individually-reasonable position sizes can add up
to an account-level risk nobody actually intended.

## A worked example

A **simulated** trader with a $20,000 account sets a per-trade risk cap of
1% ($200) and a separate account-level cap of 2% ($400) across everything
open at once. They hold a stock swing position risking $150 and a futures
position (Week 9) risking $100 simultaneously.

## The numbers

150 + 100 = $250

$250 < 400$ — the combined risk sits under the account-level cap, so both
positions can be held together under this trader's own rule. If a third,
$200-risk position were added:

250 + 200 = $450

$450 > 400$ — the account-level cap would be breached, meaning the rule
requires closing or resizing something before opening the third position,
regardless of how good any individual setup looks.

## What a diagram would show

A **comparison table** listing every instrument covered this semester —
stocks, margin/short positions, futures, options, cryptocurrency — with
columns for typical leverage, maximum loss profile, and a blank "personal
rule" column, left for a student to fill in rather than pre-filled with an
answer.

## Try it yourself

:::tip
Pick one instrument covered this semester that you have not yet written a
rule for, and draft a single risk rule for it specific enough that another
person could check, from a description of a trade, whether the rule was
followed.
:::

## The takeaway

A rulebook's value is in being specific enough to fail — a rule that can
never be checked against a real situation isn't a rule yet, just a good
intention. The Personal Market Rulebook assessment is where this gets
written down properly, instrument by instrument, for the whole semester at
once.
