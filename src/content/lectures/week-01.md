An orientation lecture: what a financial market is (a place prices form from
buyers and sellers meeting, nothing more mystical than that), a short tour of
the asset classes the course will touch — stocks, futures, options,
cryptocurrency — and why the semester moves from the slowest and simplest of
those to the fastest and most complex.

## The question this week answers

What is a market actually doing when it "sets a price," and why does this
course spend twelve weeks moving from a single stock order to leveraged
derivatives instead of just teaching every instrument at once?

## By the end of this lecture

- describe a market as a mechanism that matches buyers and sellers, not a
  force with opinions of its own
- name the asset classes this course covers, in the order it covers them
- explain why holding period and instrument complexity are the two variables
  the whole semester tracks
- list what to have ready before Session 1

## Key ideas

A **market** exists wherever there's a standing way for buyers and sellers to
meet and agree on a price — an exchange for stocks, a venue for futures and
options, an app for cryptocurrency. Nothing about a market guarantees a
"correct" price; it only produces the price the last willing buyer and seller
agreed to.

This course covers five broad **asset classes**, introduced in this order:
stocks (Weeks 2–7), leveraged stock positions via margin and short selling
(Week 8), futures (Week 9), options (Week 10), and cryptocurrency (Week 11).
That ordering isn't arbitrary — each week adds one new piece of complexity
(leverage, then a contract obligation, then a non-linear payoff) on top of
mechanics already covered, which is why later weeks keep referring back to
earlier ones.

The course's central claim, stated plainly: as holding periods get shorter
and instruments get more complex, **which direction the price moves matters
less on its own**, and execution, position sizing and risk management matter
more. Week 1 can only state that claim; Weeks 5 onward are where it gets
worked through in numbers.

## A worked example

Two students in a **simulated** market: one is willing to sell a share of a
hypothetical company "Northbank Traders" for $37.50, the other is willing to
pay up to $37.80. No trade happens until one of them agrees to the other's
number — say the buyer accepts $37.50. That single agreed price is now "the
market price," at least until the next trade. Nothing else was involved: no
committee set that number, and it says nothing yet about whether $37.50 is
good value.

## The numbers

If a student has $1,000 to spend and Northbank Traders trades at a simulated
$37.50 a share:

1,000 ÷ 37.50 = 26.67

Shares can't be bought in fractions on a typical stock order, so the
affordable amount rounds down to 26 shares, costing $975.00 and leaving
$25.00 uninvested. That leftover cash is a recurring, unglamorous fact of
trading in whole shares — it comes back when position sizing is covered
properly in Week 5.

## What a diagram would show

A **process flow diagram**: a buyer box and a seller box, both feeding into a
single "matched price" box, with a short label noting that the match only
happens when a buyer's maximum and a seller's minimum overlap. It's a
one-mechanism diagram, useful precisely because Week 1 shouldn't try to show
more than that.

## Try it yourself

:::tip
Pick any hypothetical share price between $10 and $200 and a hypothetical
budget between $200 and $2,000. Work out how many whole shares that budget
buys, the total cost, and the leftover cash — the same calculation as above,
with your own numbers.
:::

## The takeaway

A market is a matching mechanism, not an oracle. Everything else this
semester covers is really a question of what happens around that one matched
price: how it's reached (Week 2), what to do with the observations it
produces (Weeks 3–4), and how much of your own money to risk on a view about
where it goes next (Week 5 onward).
