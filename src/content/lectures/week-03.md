Investing and trading are often talked about as if they were the same
activity at different speeds. This lecture treats holding period as the
variable that decides which costs and risks actually apply to a position,
and surveys the styles the rest of the semester will name explicitly —
buy-and-hold investing, swing trading, day trading — without yet teaching
how to do any of them.

## The question this week answers

Holding period isn't just a style label — it decides how often the
execution friction from Week 2 gets paid and which risks from Week 5 apply
at all. What actually separates "investing" from "trading" along that axis?

## By the end of this lecture

- use holding period as the organising axis for the rest of the course
- compare investing, swing trading and day trading on the same two axes:
  typical holding period and primary goal
- explain why a style is a fit-to-goals question, not a best-versus-worst one
- locate where each style reappears later in the semester

## Key ideas

**Holding period** — how long a position is typically kept — is the single
variable this course tracks most closely, because so much else (which costs
matter, which risks dominate, how much attention a position needs) changes
with it. A position held for years behaves differently, mechanically, from
one held for hours, independent of which one is "smarter." Shorten the
holding period and the spread from Week 2 gets paid more often; the account
also spends more time exposed to the drawdown risk Week 5 quantifies.

Three styles get named this semester: **investing** (long holding periods,
usually years, goal is typically long-run growth or income), **swing
trading** (holding periods of days to weeks, Week 6), and **day trading**
(positions opened and closed within a single day, Week 7). None of these is
presented as superior — they suit different goals, amounts of available time,
and tolerance for the costs and risks that Week 5 covers.

## A worked example

Three **simulated** students each start with $10,000. One buys shares in a
diversified fund and checks the account twice a year. One opens and closes a
handful of stock positions a month, holding each one to a week or two. One
opens and closes positions within the trading day, holding nothing overnight.
Same starting capital, three entirely different sets of concerns — none of
the three is described here as the "right" one.

## The numbers

Holding period implies roughly how many trades a style produces in a year.
With about 250 trading days in a year, a rough estimate of trade frequency is:

trades per year ≈ 250 ÷ average holding period in days

- average hold of 180 days (long-term investing): 250 ÷ 180 ≈ 1.4
  trades a year
- average hold of 5 days (swing trading): 250 ÷ 5 = 50 trades a year
- average hold of 1 day (day trading): up to 250 trades a year

This is a rough estimate, not a rule — but it shows something real: shorter
holding periods multiply how many times execution costs like the spread from
Week 2 get paid.

## What a diagram would show

A **timeline**: three horizontal bars of very different lengths (years, weeks,
hours) placed on the same axis, labelled investing, swing trading and day
trading, making the holding-period difference visible at a glance rather than
just stated in words.

## Try it yourself

:::tip
Given a description of a **simulated** trade log entry — "bought Monday,
sold the following Wednesday" — classify it as investing, swing trading or
day trading using holding period alone, then note what information you'd
still need (goal, position size, how it fits a broader plan) before judging
whether it was a sensible trade.
:::

## The takeaway

Style is a fit question, answered by time available, goals and temperament —
not a ranking. What holding period does settle, unambiguously, is how often
the mechanical costs from Week 2 and the risk tools from Week 5 get applied —
shorter holds mean more frequent exposure to both, which is exactly why the
rest of the course is organised around it.
