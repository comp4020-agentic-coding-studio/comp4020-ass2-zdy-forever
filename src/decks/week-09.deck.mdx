## The question this week answers

What happens when leverage is not an optional account setting, but a
structural feature of the contract itself?

The point is not to find a "best" trading style or instrument. It is to
understand what can make the account fail.

## With futures, a small margin deposit can control a much larger notional position — the account can move far more than the underlying price does

---

## A worked example

The course uses futures as an embedded-leverage case study, not as a survey
of commodities or trading strategies.

A **simulated** futures contract represents 100 units of an underlying
priced at $50 — a notional value of $5,000. The exchange requires $500 of
initial margin to hold one contract. The contract's payoff is linear: each
unit of price movement changes P/L by a fixed amount.

---

## The numbers: leverage multiple

<div class="calc-block">
  notional value = 100 × $50 = $5,000
  <br />
  leverage multiple = 5,000 ÷ 500 = <strong>10x</strong>
</div>

---

## The numbers: what a small move does

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">A 4% move in the underlying ($2)</span>
  <p>P/L = 100 × $2 = $200</p>
  <p>200 ÷ 500 = <strong>40%</strong> of the margin actually put up</p>
</div>

A 4% move in the underlying produced a 40% move relative to the trader's own
capital — the same leverage principle from Week 8, now embedded in the
contract design itself.

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>A futures contract is an obligation, not a choice.</li>
  <li>The exchange-set margin determines the leverage built into the exposure.</li>
  <li>Linear payoff makes the P/L easy to calculate — but leverage makes the account impact large.</li>
  <li>Week 10 adds nonlinear payoff and premium loss on top of the derivative framework.</li>
</ol>
