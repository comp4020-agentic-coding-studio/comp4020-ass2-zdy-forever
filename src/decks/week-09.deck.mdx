# Futures

## Week 9 · The first derivative instrument

## The question this week answers

What is a futures contract actually obligating a trader to do, and how does
Week 8's leverage show up here without a separate margin account decision?

## A contract, not a stock

A **futures contract** — a standardised agreement to buy or sell a set
quantity of something at a set price on a set future date. No ownership
changes hands today, only an obligation that settles later or is closed out
before then.

This course treats futures generically, as a contract shape — not a
commodities course.

## Futures don't use a margin *setting* the way Week 8's stock example did — the exchange sets it, and it's usually a much smaller fraction of the position

---

## A worked example

A **simulated** futures contract represents 100 units of an underlying
priced at $50 — a notional value of $5,000. The exchange requires $500 of
initial margin to hold one contract.

---

## The numbers: leverage multiple

<div class="calc-block">
  notional value = 100 × $50 = $5,000
  <br />
  leverage multiple = 5,000 ÷ 500 = <strong>10x</strong>
</div>

---

## The numbers: what a small move does

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">A 4% move in the underlying ($2)</span>
  <p>P/L = 100 × $2 = $200</p>
  <p>200 ÷ 500 = <strong>40%</strong> of the margin actually put up</p>
</div>

A 4% move in the underlying produced a 40% move relative to the trader's own
capital — the leverage multiple doing exactly what it says.

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>A futures contract is an obligation, not a choice — unlike an option, it can't simply be left unused.</li>
  <li>Its leverage is fixed by the exchange's margin requirement, not chosen account-by-account like Week 8's stock margin.</li>
  <li>Its payoff is a straight line, moving by a constant amount per unit of price change either way.</li>
  <li>Week 10 keeps this obligation-versus-right distinction in view while introducing a payoff that bends.</li>
</ol>
