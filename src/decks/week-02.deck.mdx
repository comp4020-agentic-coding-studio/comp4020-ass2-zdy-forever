## The question this week answers

Between clicking "buy" and actually owning a share, where can execution
differ from the price on screen — and why does that matter more when
capital is limited?

The point is not to find a "best" trading style or instrument. It is to
understand what can make the account fail.

## Two jobs, two account types

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Broker</h3>
    <p>Takes the order from the trader and routes it onward.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Exchange</h3>
    <p>Matches the order against other orders in the market.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Cash account</h3>
    <p>Can only spend capital already in the account.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Margin account</h3>
    <p>Can borrow against account equity — leverage appears in full in Week 8.</p>
  </div>
</div>

A single app can make these feel like one step. They aren't.

---

## Every order quotes two prices at once

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Market order</h3>
    <p>Fills immediately at the best available price. Price is <em>not</em>
    guaranteed.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Limit order</h3>
    <p>Fills only at a chosen price or better. Price is guaranteed — the
    order might never fill at all.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The **bid** is the highest price a buyer currently offers; the **ask** is
the lowest a seller currently accepts.

## The quoted price is information — the executable price is what changes the account

---

## A worked example

Simulated stock "ABC" is quoted:

**bid $50.00 / ask $50.05**

A student places a market buy order for 10 shares. A market buy fills
against the ask — the price sellers are willing to accept right now — not
the bid, and not some average of the two.

---

## The numbers

<div class="calc-block">
  10 shares × $50.05 ask = $500.50
  <br />
  <br />
  Mid-price = ($50.00 + $50.05) ÷ 2 = $50.025
  <br />
  10 shares × $50.025 = $500.25
  <br />
  <br />
  $500.50 − $500.25 = $0.25 — the cost of certainty
</div>

Trivial on 10 shares. It stops looking trivial once Week 7 covers paying
the same spread dozens of times a day.

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>Broker and exchange are different parts of the execution chain.</li>
  <li>Market orders exchange price certainty for immediacy; limit orders do the reverse.</li>
  <li>The spread is paid before the market direction has had any chance to help.</li>
  <li>For a small account, execution quality is part of risk management, not an administrative detail.</li>
</ol>
