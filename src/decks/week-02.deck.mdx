# How a Stock Trade Actually Happens

## Week 2 · Stocks, exchanges, brokers, accounts, and order types

---

## The question this week answers

Between clicking "buy" and actually owning a share, several separate
systems do separate jobs.

What are they — and why can the price you see when you click not be the
price you pay?

---

## By the end of this lecture

- name the separate roles an exchange and a broker each play in a trade
- distinguish a cash account from a margin account
- explain why a market order and a limit order can fill at different
  prices
- trace a simulated order from click to fill

---

## Two different jobs

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Broker</h3>
    <p>Who a trader deals with directly. Takes the order and routes it
    onward.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Exchange</h3>
    <p>Where the order actually meets other orders and gets matched.</p>
  </div>
</div>

A single app can make these feel like one step. They aren't.

---

## Two account types

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Cash account</h3>
    <p>Can only spend money already in it.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Margin account</h3>
    <p>Can borrow against what's already held — what makes leverage and

  </div>

## Every order quotes two prices at once

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Market order</h3>
    <p>Fills immediately at the best available price. Price is <em>not</em>
    guaranteed.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Limit order</h3>
    <p>Fills only at a chosen price or better. Price is guaranteed — the
    order might never fill at all.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The **bid** is the highest price a buyer currently offers; the **ask** is
the lowest a seller currently accepts.

---

## A worked example

Simulated stock "ABC" is quoted:

**bid $50.00 / ask $50.05**

A student places a market buy order for 10 shares. A market buy fills
against the ask — the price sellers are willing to accept right now — not
the bid, and not some average of the two.

---

## The numbers

<div class="calc-block">
  10 shares × $50.05 ask = $500.50
  <br />
  <br />
  Mid-price = ($50.00 + $50.05) ÷ 2 = $50.025
  <br />
  10 shares × $50.025 = $500.25
  <br />
  <br />
  $500.50 − $500.25 = $0.25 — the cost of certainty
</div>

Trivial on 10 shares. It stops looking trivial once Week 7 covers paying
the same spread dozens of times a day.

---

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>An exchange matches; a broker routes. Different jobs.</li>
  <li>A margin account can borrow; a cash account can't.</li>
  <li>Market orders trade price certainty for execution certainty — limit orders trade the reverse.</li>
  <li>Neither order type says anything about whether the stock is a good idea.</li>
</ol>
