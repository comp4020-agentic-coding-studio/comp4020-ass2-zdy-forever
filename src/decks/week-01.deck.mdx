## The question this week answers

What changes when the account is small enough that every spread, sizing
decision, drawdown and forced exit materially reduces the room to keep
trading?

The point is not to find a "best" trading style or instrument. It is to
understand what can make the account fail.

## Why "small account" is its own problem

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">

  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Drawdown asymmetry</h3>
    <p>Losses shrink the capital base; recovery requires a disproportionately larger gain.</p>

## The first objective is not maximising return — it is preserving enough capital to remain able to act

---

## A market still just matches buyers and sellers

A market is any standing way for buyers and sellers to meet and agree on a
price — a stock exchange, a futures or options venue, a crypto app. None of
them guarantee a "correct" price; each only produces the price the last
willing buyer and seller agreed to.

<div class="timeline">
  <div class="timeline-step">
    <h3>Seller</h3>
    <p>Simulated "Northbank Traders" — will sell for $37.50</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-step">
    <h3>Buyer</h3>
    <p>Will pay up to $37.80</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-step">
    <h3>Match</h3>
    <p>Buyer accepts $37.50 — that is now "the market price"</p>
  </div>
</div>

Simulated prices. Nothing about that match says whether $37.50 is "good
value" — only that two sides agreed.

---

## Whole-share constraints show up immediately

<div class="calc-block">
  $1,000 budget ÷ $37.50 a share = 26.67 shares
  <br />
  <br />
  Shares trade in whole units → 26 shares
  <br />
  <br />
  26 × $37.50 = $975.00 spent
  <br />
  <br />
  $1,000 − $975.00 = $25.00 left over, uninvested
</div>

That leftover cash is small here. Later weeks show how contract sizes and
margin can make sizing constraints much larger.

crypto — each is now a case study in a different failure mechanism. The
instrument changes; the course keeps asking the same question: how much
room does the account have before friction, loss or forced closure takes
control?

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>A market sets a matched price; it does not protect a trader from bad sizing or execution.</li>
  <li>Small accounts feel frictions and drawdowns more quickly because there is less capital buffer.</li>
  <li>The course now follows one question across every instrument: what reduces the account's ability to survive?</li>
  <li>Stocks are the baseline; later weeks add leverage, contract obligations, nonlinear payoffs and liquidation risk.</li>
</ol>
