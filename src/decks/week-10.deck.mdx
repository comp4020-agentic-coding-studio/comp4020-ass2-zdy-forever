## A futures contract obligates both sides. An option's buyer can simply let it expire unused

---

## Calls, puts, and the cost of a right

A **call option** — the right, not the obligation, to buy an underlying at a
set **strike price** on or before **expiry**.

A **put option** — the same right, to sell instead.

That right isn't free: it costs a **premium**, paid upfront — also the
buyer's maximum possible loss.

---

## A genuine structural difference, at a cost

If the option expires with no value, the premium is gone and nothing further
is owed — a bounded loss, unlike Week 8's short position, which had no
ceiling at all.

The premium is still a real, certain expense regardless of outcome. A
smaller price move than expected can still mean losing all of it.

---

## A worked example

A **simulated** trader buys one call option contract (100 shares) on a
stock, strike price $50, premium $2 per share ($200 total).

---

## The numbers: payoff above the strike

<div class="calc-block">
  Stock at $55 at expiry:
  <br />
  (55 − 50) × 100 = $500 intrinsic value − $200 premium
  <br />
  = <strong>$300 net profit</strong>
  <br />
  <br />
  Breakeven = strike + premium = 50 + 2 = <strong>$52</strong>
</div>

---

## The numbers: payoff below the strike

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">Stock at $48 at expiry</span>
  <p>The option has no value — the loss is simply the $200 premium paid</p>
  <p>The same loss as if the stock had fallen to $30, or to $1</p>
</div>

Unlike Week 8's short position, the loss doesn't grow as price moves further
against it — capped at what was paid upfront.

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>An option is a right; a futures contract is an obligation — the buyer can let an option expire unused.</li>
  <li>The premium is a certain cost, paid whether or not the option is ever exercised.</li>
  <li>A bounded loss is a real structural difference from a short position — not a reason to treat options as automatically lower-risk.</li>
  <li>Week 11 applies this same leverage-and-payoff thinking to a market with its own custody and exchange structure.</li>
</ol>
