## The question this week answers

How does a payoff that bends around strike and expiry change what "risk"
means for a small account?

The point is not to find a "best" trading style or instrument. It is to
understand what can make the account fail.

## "Maximum loss is capped" can sound safe — losing 100% of the option premium is still a complete loss of the capital committed to that position

---

## Three ideas that change the risk intuition

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Right, not obligation</h3>
    <p>The buyer can let the option expire unused, unlike a futures obligation</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Premium</h3>
    <p>Paid upfront; for the buyer it is the maximum possible loss — and can be lost entirely</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Nonlinear payoff</h3>
    <p>The relationship between underlying price and option P/L changes around the strike and expiry</p>
  </div>
</div>

Options change the shape of risk rather than removing it. The size of the
premium position still has to fit the account's survival rules.

---

## A worked example

A **simulated** trader buys one call option contract (100 shares) on a
stock, strike price $50, premium $2 per share ($200 total). At expiry, the
option only has intrinsic value above the strike — the premium must also be
recovered before the position is profitable.

---

## The numbers: payoff above the strike

<div class="calc-block">
  Stock at $55 at expiry:
  <br />
  (55 − 50) × 100 = $500 intrinsic value − $200 premium
  <br />
  = <strong>$300 net profit</strong>
  <br />
  <br />
  Breakeven = strike + premium = 50 + 2 = <strong>$52</strong>
</div>

---

## The numbers: payoff below the strike

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">Stock at $48 at expiry</span>
  <p>The option expires worthless — the loss is the full $200 premium</p>
  <p>The same loss as if the stock had fallen to $30, or to $1</p>
</div>

Bounded loss is structurally different from an uncapped short — but the
option buyer can still lose every dollar of premium paid.

---

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>Options are rights; futures are obligations.</li>
  <li>The premium is a certain upfront cost and can be lost entirely.</li>
  <li>The payoff is nonlinear, so stock intuition does not transfer cleanly.</li>
  <li>For a small account, the premium still needs a size limit just like any other risk budget.</li>
</ol>
