## Week 12 · Rules that keep one bad position from ending the account

## The question this week answers

Which rules are specific enough to protect the account when execution,
drawdown, leverage or instrument complexity turns against it?

The point is not to find a "best" trading style or instrument. It is to
understand what can make the account fail.

---

## By the end of this lecture

<ol class="objective-list">
  <li>write risk rules that are checkable, not just aspirational</li>
  <li>set both a per-trade risk cap and an account-level risk cap</li>
  <li>define limits on leverage and which instruments the account can use</li>
  <li>state the conditions under which trading stops altogether</li>
</ol>

---

## A survival rulebook is a set of constraints

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Per-trade risk cap</h3>
    <p>The maximum account capital that can be lost on one planned position</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Account-level cap</h3>
    <p>The maximum combined risk across every open position at once</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Execution rules</h3>
    <p>When a market or limit order is acceptable, and when liquidity is too thin</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Leverage / instrument limits</h3>
    <p>The maximum leverage, and which products the account is allowed to use</p>
  </div>
</div>

## A rule only matters if someone else could look at a trade and say whether it was followed or broken

---

## A worked example

A **simulated** trader, $20,000 account: a 1% per-trade cap ($200) and a
separate 2% account-level cap ($400) across everything open at once. They
hold a stock swing position risking $150 and a Week 9 futures position
risking $100, simultaneously.

---

## The numbers: two positions together

<div class="calc-block">
  150 + 100 = $250
  <br />
  $250 &lt; $400 — under the account-level cap, both positions can be held
  together
</div>

---

## The numbers: a third position breaks the rule

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">Adding a third, $200-risk position</span>
  <p>250 + 200 = $450</p>
  <p>$450 &gt; $400 — the account-level cap is breached</p>
</div>

With just the first two positions, $150 of the $400 budget sat unused — that
headroom is what the rule is built around, not a mistake. The rule requires
closing or resizing something before the third position can open, regardless
of how good that setup looks on its own.

## Try it yourself

<p class="activity-label">Draft a rule</p>

Pick one instrument covered this semester.

Write one rule for position size, one for execution, and one condition
that stops trading altogether. Each rule must be precise enough that
another person could mark it "followed" or "broken."

---

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>A rulebook is a set of constraints, not a summary of the course.</li>
  <li>Per-trade risk isn't enough — open positions interact at the account level.</li>
  <li>Leverage, execution and instrument access should all have explicit limits.</li>
  <li>The goal isn't eliminating losses. It's stopping ordinary mistakes from ending the account's ability to continue.</li>
</ol>
