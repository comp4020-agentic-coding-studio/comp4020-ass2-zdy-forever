## A rule that can't be checked against a specific situation isn't finished yet

---

## What a rulebook actually is

Not a summary of what was taught — a set of decisions:

- which instruments from Weeks 1-11 you'd actually use
- how you'd size a position — Week 5's method still applies, regardless of
  instrument
- what would invalidate a position
- under what conditions you'd stop trading altogether, even temporarily

---

## Rules have to work together, not just individually

A risk budget usually caps total risk across every open position at once —
not just risk per trade.

Several individually-reasonable position sizes can add up to an
account-level risk nobody actually intended.

---

## A worked example

A **simulated** trader, $20,000 account: a 1% per-trade cap ($200) and a
separate 2% account-level cap ($400) across everything open at once. They
hold a stock swing position risking $150 and a Week 9 futures position
risking $100, simultaneously.

---

## The numbers: two positions together

<div class="calc-block">
  150 + 100 = $250
  <br />
  $250 &lt; $400 — under the account-level cap, both positions can be held
  together
</div>

---

## The numbers: a third position breaks the rule

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">Adding a third, $200-risk position</span>
  <p>250 + 200 = $450</p>
  <p>$450 &gt; $400 — the account-level cap is breached</p>
</div>

The rule requires closing or resizing something first — regardless of how
good the third setup looks on its own.

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>A rulebook is a set of decisions, not a summary of what was taught.</li>
  <li>Risk rules need to work together — an account-level cap, not just a per-trade one.</li>
  <li>A rule's value is in being specific enough to fail — an aspiration isn't a rule yet.</li>
  <li>The Personal Market Rulebook assessment is where this gets written down properly, instrument by instrument, for the whole semester at once.</li>
</ol>
