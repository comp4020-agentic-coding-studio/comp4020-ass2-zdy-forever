## The question this week answers

When the account controls more exposure than it can fully fund, how small
can an adverse price move be before the broker takes control?

The point is not to find a "best" trading style or instrument. It is to
understand what can make the account fail.

## Borrowing changes the account, not the stock

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Margin</h3>
    <p>Borrowing part of a position's cost, using account equity as collateral.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Leverage</h3>
    <p>Total position size divided by the trader's own capital in it.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Short selling</h3>
    <p>Borrow, sell, buy back later — the loss has no theoretical ceiling.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Margin call</h3>
    <p>Forced action once equity falls below the broker's maintenance requirement.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Leverage doesn't just magnify losses — it shortens how much adverse movement the account can survive before a broker closes the position for it

---

## A worked example

A **simulated** $10,000 stock position, split $5,000 trader capital and
$5,000 margin loan (2x leverage), against a 25% maintenance margin
requirement. The loan stays fixed while equity shrinks as the position
falls — the position doesn't need to reach zero for the account to be in
trouble.

---

## The numbers: a margin call trigger

<div class="calc-block">
  equity = position value − $5,000 loan
  <br />
  margin call when equity = 25% × position value
  <br />
  <br />
  position − 5,000 = 0.25 × position
  <br />
  0.75 × position = 5,000 → position = $6,666.67
</div>

$6,666.67 ÷ $10,000 = 66.7% of the original value — a **33.3% price drop**
triggers this call, well before the position is worth zero. The exact
threshold depends on the broker's own rules; the example isolates the
leverage mechanism.

## Long vs. short, side by side

<div class="comparison-grid">
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Long position</h3>
    <p>Max gain: unlimited. Max loss: 100% — price can't go below zero.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="comparison-card">
    <h3>Short position</h3>
    <p>Max gain: 100% — price can't go below zero. Max loss: unlimited.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Short selling — borrow shares, sell them, buy them back later. Profits if
price falls; the loss has no ceiling if it rises instead.

---

## The numbers: an uncapped short loss

<div class="risk-callout">
  <span class="risk-callout-label">Simulated short, 100 shares at $20</span>
  <p>Price doubles to $40 → buy-back costs $4,000 → $2,000 loss (100% of proceeds)</p>
  <p>Price triples to $60 → buy-back costs $6,000 → $4,000 loss (200% of proceeds)</p>
</div>

There is no price at which the loss stops growing — unlike a long position
bought outright.

## The takeaway

<ol class="recap-list">
  <li>Leverage multiplies both gains and losses relative to the trader's own capital.</li>
  <li>A margin call is the mechanical consequence of leverage, not an edge case.</li>
  <li>A short position's loss has no ceiling, unlike a long position's 100% cap.</li>
  <li>Weeks 9 through 11 keep this same forced-exit logic, just embedded in different instruments and venues.</li>
</ol>
